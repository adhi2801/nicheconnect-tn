# Survival playbook: how startups like ours die, and the rules that keep us alive

Written 10 October 2026, at Adhi's request: "70%+ of startups fail in India; help us survive, and earn well, gradually, like a real business." Research, not law: the founders decide every rule here. It sits beside `GO_TO_MARKET.md` (how we win the first city) and `REVENUE_RESEARCH.md` (how we earn), and does not repeat them.

**The honest frame.** Over 11,000 Indian startups shut down in 2025, 30% more than in 2024 (Tracxn, via Inc42). Most did not die of bad code. They died of four things:
- running out of cash before the business paid for itself;
- building what nobody needed enough to pay for;
- founders falling out;
- costs growing faster than revenue.

Every rule below answers one of those four. None of them is about working harder.

---

## 1. The four ways we could die, and the rule against each

| How startups die | Our rule |
|---|---|
| **Out of cash** | **Stay default alive** (Paul Graham, 2015): at our current spending and growth, we reach profit before the money runs out, without needing to raise. We check it monthly (section 4). Spending that pushes us into "default dead" needs both founders |
| **Nobody needs it enough to pay** | **Paying brands before polish.** Revenue stream 1 in `REVENUE_RESEARCH.md` (done-for-you campaigns) brings cash and truth in the first months. Interviews follow the Mom Test: ask about their past, not our idea, and count commitments (time, money, an introduction), never compliments |
| **Founders fall out** | **Agree before it matters.** A founders' agreement with roles, vesting (4 years, 1-year cliff is the common pattern), what happens if one leaves, and every IP assigned to the company (`docs/standards/legal.md` section 2). Decisions in writing (`docs/DECISIONS.md`, which we already keep). A weekly founders' hour for money, numbers and disagreements, so none of them festers |
| **Costs outrun revenue** | **No fixed cost without a reason in revenue.** No office, no staff and no paid tools until a number in section 4 asks for them. Cloud costs stay on free tiers and credits until the pilot pays (`GO_TO_MARKET.md` section 9) |

## 2. How a marketplace like ours wins (the lessons that apply)

From *The Cold Start Problem* (Andrew Chen), Paul Graham's essays, *The Mom Test* (Rob Fitzpatrick), and a16z's marketplace research. Each lesson is mapped to what we already do or must do.

1. **Start with one atomic network**: the smallest group that works on its own. For us that is one city, one niche and the brands that hire it: Coimbatore food first (`GO_TO_MARKET.md`). We win it completely before the next.
2. **Win the hard side first.** For us it is **brands with budgets**, not creators: creators come where paying brands are. Founder time goes to brands.
3. **Do things that don't scale.** Recruit the first 20 brands by hand. Run their first campaigns for them (stream 1), and phone every creator after their first deal. What we learn becomes the product; the backend already records each step to learn from.
4. **Liquidity is the product.** A marketplace is worth exactly how likely a brand's campaign is to fill, and how likely a creator's application is to turn into a deal. We measure both weekly (section 4), and they outrank every feature request.
5. **Repeat deals are where value compounds.** A brand that books the same creator again proves the whole thing works. "Work together again" (D-084) exists for this; repeat rate is a headline number.
6. **Trust is the moat.** Competitors can copy features. They cannot copy years of honest records: the deal record (D-057), delivery and payment records (D-038, D-034), and the line we never cross (`PSYCHOLOGY_AND_TRUST.md`). Anything that would spend that trust for a short-term number is refused.
7. **Watch for the anti-network effect**: spam and scams that make the network worse as it grows. `docs/standards/trust-and-safety.md` is how we stop it, before it starts.
8. **Beat the best competitor at each thing, and have what none has.** `CLAUDE.md` section 7.0 and `docs/COMPETITIVE_LANDSCAPE.md` hold us to it; each feature names the competitor it beats.

## 3. Money we can get without selling much of the company

For two student founders in Tamil Nadu, in order of cost to us:

| Source | What it gives | What it costs | Fits when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue (stream 1) | Cash, and proof that people pay | Our time | From the first month: the best money there is |
| **Startup India Seed Fund** | Grant up to ₹20 lakh for proof of concept; debt up to ₹50 lakh later. Through a DPIIT-empanelled incubator; 3–6 months to approve | **No equity, no repayment** for the grant | Once incorporated and DPIIT-recognised, within 2 years of incorporation |
| **StartupTN TANSEED** | ₹10 lakh (up to ₹15 lakh for some categories), plus a year-long accelerator | A 3% stake | Registered with StartupTN (TANSIM) and Startup India, based in Tamil Nadu |
| A Coimbatore or Chennai incubator | Mentors, an address, and the route to the Seed Fund | Usually a small stake or fee: read terms | Before applying to the Seed Fund |
| Cloud credits (AWS Activate and similar) | Months of hosting | Nothing, if we stay under them | Now |
| Angel or VC money | Speed | Equity, and their timeline replaces ours | Only once the numbers in section 4 show the machine works, and only if speed is what we lack |

Every figure here is from public pages read on 10 October 2026, and eligibility changes: confirm each with the scheme before applying. Incorporation (`legal.md` section 2) comes first, because nearly all of them need it.

## 4. The numbers we watch every week (and the backend must give us)

Every number comes from the records, never from memory (`CLAUDE.md` section 7.0). **Item 63 in `docs/BACKEND_COMPLETE.md`** builds them as an admin view. Until then, the founders count by hand from the admin and the export.

| Number | What it tells us | Healthy direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Campaign fill rate**: open campaigns that reach an agreed deal within 14 days | Liquidity, brand side: does posting here work? | Up; the headline number |
| **Application success**: applications that become agreed deals | Liquidity, creator side | Up, but not by cutting applications |
| **Time to first application** on a new campaign | How alive the marketplace feels to a brand | Down |
| **Repeat rate**: brands that book again within 90 days | Whether we are useful or a one-off | Up |
| **Paid on time**: payments confirmed by the due date | Whether the trust promise holds | Up, toward all of them |
| **Active brands and creators** in the atomic network | Density | Up, in one city before the next |
| **Revenue, costs, cash, and months of runway** | Default alive or dead | Runway never under 6 months without a plan written down |

## 5. Earning more than our classmates: gradually, and for real

A placement offer pays from month one. A company pays nothing at first, and can pay far more later. That only happens if it survives, and ownership is where most of the money is made. So:

1. **Stage 0, now to the first paying brands:** no founder salary. Living costs come from outside the company. Every rupee earned stays in the company.
2. **Stage 1, default alive and revenue covering costs:** founders draw enough to live on, the same for both, set by both. The point is to stop living on savings, not to get rich.
3. **Stage 2, profitable or funded, with section 4 healthy:** founder pay moves toward what a strong engineer earns. Real wealth comes from the value of what each founder owns, which is why **vesting and the founders' agreement come first**.
4. **A decision date, written down.** Agree now on what the numbers must show by when (for example, a set number of paying brands by the end of the pilot). On that date, both founders decide in writing: continue, change course, or stop. It is not pessimism. Without a date, a company dies slowly instead of changing in time.
